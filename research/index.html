Pillar 1

Funding gaps: who gets funded and why

The baseline your simulator starts from. These papers separate "fewer women and minorities get funded" from "investors are biased", which are different claims.

Same pitch, different voice

Across real pitch competitions and two experiments, investors preferred identical pitches narrated by men. Attractiveness boosted men and made no difference for women.

Brooks, Huang, Kearney & Murray (2014), PNAS 111(12)

For you: clean evidence of bias in the human signal any model would learn from.

Promotion vs. prevention questions

Investors tended to ask men about potential gains and women about avoiding losses, a pattern the authors tie to the funding gap. The paper won AMJ's best paper award for 2019.

Kanze, Huang, Conley & Higgins (2018), Academy of Management Journal 61(2), 586–614

For you: a mechanism you can listen for in VC interviews, and a feature an LLM screener could inherit from pitch transcripts.

Male investors, female founders

On AngelList, women were less successful with male investors than similar men. Where women did get male backing, those startups did as well or better, which points to taste-based bias.

Ewens & Townsend (2020), Journal of Financial Economics 135(3), 653–677

For you: the "chicken and egg" point. Most investors and past founders are men, so historical data starts tilted.

Networks, not just judgments

At HBS's venture competition, exposure to more VC judges helped men start VC-backed firms but not women. Men followed up with judges far more often; judges responded equally.

Howell & Nanda (2024), JFQA 59(6), 2733–2761

For you: bias can enter at sourcing, before any screener sees the deal. Worth a variable in the model.

Birds of a feather

VCs who share ethnicity, school or past employer co-invest more, and those affinity-based syndicates perform worse, especially early stage.

Gompers, Mukharlyamov & Xuan (2016), Journal of Financial Economics 119(3)

For you: homophily has a measurable cost. Supports the school-prestige dimension.

Venture bearding

Law scholars name the practice of founders masking stigmatized identity to raise, from changing how they present to adding a male co-founder to front the raise.

Edwards & McGinley (2019), UC Davis Law Review 52, 1873

For you: the term Dr. Pearl raised. Also a data problem: bearded teams look male-led in datasets.

Pillar 2

AI and algorithmic screening in VC

The closest literature to your question. It already shows AI can both widen and narrow who gets funded, which is why a multi-cycle view matters.

AI makes VCs pick the familiar

After VC firms adopt AI screening, they shift toward startups resembling ones already tried. They get fewer failures but also fewer rare breakout wins.

Bonelli (2023), "Data-driven Investors", HEC Paris working paper

For you: the single most relevant paper. Backward-looking similarity is exactly the prototype effect you want to model.

VCs think in stereotypes; algorithms might not

On French data covering VC-backed and non-backed firms, an ML model found VCs pass on predictably good founders. Algorithm-picked firms had more women, younger founders and more non-Paris founders.

Lyonnet & Stern, "Machine Learning About Venture Capital Choices" (2025 draft; earlier "Venture Capital (Mis)Allocation in the Age of AI"), R&R at Review of Financial Studies

Counterpoint A model trained on all new firms can reduce bias. The training set is the lever.

What VCs say they weigh

Surveying roughly 885 institutional VCs, the team was rated most important for selection, ahead of product or market. Most deals came through networks; about 10% were inbound.

Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan & Strebulaev (2020), Journal of Financial Economics 135(1), 169–190

For you: if "team" dominates, founder background has a direct path into any model trained on VC choices. Tests your traction hypothesis.

Human vs. model screening

An Earlybird partner benchmarked a screening model against over a hundred human investors. His firm now uses AI for top-of-funnel screening with humans deciding.

Retterath (2020), SSRN working paper

Verify Coverage gives 111 vs. 114 investors and accuracy of 80% vs. 57% only via a German press summary. Read the paper before citing numbers. He'd also be a strong interview.

LLMs and women founders

A 2025 study found LLMs leaned toward masculine traits over feminine ones specifically in investment scenarios.

"Detecting Gender Stereotype Biases Against Women Entrepreneurs in LLMs", Journal of Business Ethics 207 (2026)

For you: the off-the-shelf LLM layer (the "pattern-matching" firm you worked with) may carry its own bias. Authors not captured in my search; pull the paper.

Names change LLM advice

An audit across 42 prompt templates found GPT-4 and other models gave worse advice to names associated with women and racial minorities. Numeric anchors in the prompt reduced the gap.

Salinas, Haim & Nyarko (2025), Stanford Law, arXiv 2402.14875

For you: a ready intervention to test: does giving the screener traction numbers shrink demographic effects?

Pillar 3

Feedback-loop bias and finance analogs

The methodological backbone of your simulator. None of these are about VC, which is part of the point.

Runaway feedback loops

A model that sends police where past arrests were recorded only observes crime where it sends police, so it keeps sending them back regardless of true crime rates. The authors prove this with simple urn models and test fixes.

Ensign, Friedler, Neville, Scheidegger & Venkatasubramanian (2018), FAT* / PMLR 81

For you: your blueprint. Swap "neighborhoods" for founder groups and "arrests" for exits. Their simulation code is public on GitHub (algofairness/runaway-feedback-loops-src).

Selective labels

When human decisions decide which cases get outcomes (bail, treatment), the data only shows results for those chosen. Comparing a model to humans on that data misleads.

Lakkaraju, Kleinberg, Leskovec, Ludwig & Mullainathan (2017), KDD

For you: unfunded startups never get an exit label. This names the core data problem in VC and justifies a simulation.

ML in credit: better accuracy, unequal gains

On ~10M US mortgages, ML predicted default better than logistic models, but Black and Hispanic borrowers were less likely to benefit, and rate disparity widened in their market model.

Fuster, Goldsmith-Pinkham, Ramadorai & Walther (2022), Journal of Finance 77(1), 5–47

For you: the credit case for your "AI across financial infrastructure" framing. Accuracy and fairness can diverge.

Fintech lending discrimination

Latinx and African-American borrowers paid higher rates on equivalent mortgages, about $765M a year. Algorithmic lenders cut the gap by over a third and showed no discrimination in rejections.

Bartlett, Morse, Stanton & Wallace (2022 version; NBER w25943)

Counterpoint Algorithms can reduce human bias. Your simulator should allow that outcome too.

Pillar 4

Cognitive-science foundations

What makes this a Cog Sci thesis rather than a finance one. Grading criteria reward understanding of background literature and original ideas; this is where yours lives.

Prototype theory

Categories are organized around their best examples, and members are judged by resemblance to that prototype rather than by strict definitions.

Rosch (1975), J. Exp. Psych: General 104(3), 192–233; Rosch & Mervis (1975), Cognitive Psychology 7

For you: "fundable founder" as a category whose prototype was set by past winners. A model trained on exits learns that prototype.

Representativeness

People judge probability by similarity to a stereotype and neglect base rates.

Tversky & Kahneman (1974), Science 185(4157), 1124–1131

For you: the classic link between prototypes and investment error.

Stereotypes with a kernel of truth

A formal model where people overweight traits that are more common in one group than another. The result is often roughly accurate but exaggerated.

Bordalo, Coffman, Gennaioli & Shleifer (2016), QJE 131(4), 1753–1794

For you: Lyonnet & Stern apply this exact model to VCs. It bridges Pillar 4 to Pillar 2.

Gut feel is not random

Angel investors' gut feel blended intuition and analysis. It produced fewer hits overall but bigger ones.

Huang & Pearce (2015), Administrative Science Quarterly 60(4), 634–670

For you: what the conversational firm you worked with does. What does a model lose when it replaces this?

Evidence against the simple bias story

Your stated hypothesis is to test bias, not presuppose it. These findings keep the thesis honest, and a reviewer will expect you to address them.

Cold emails favored women

80,000 fictitious pitch emails to 28,000 investors: female-named founders got about 9% more interested replies than men with identical pitches, and Asian-named founders about 6% more than White-named.

Gornall & Strebulaev (2025), Management Science

The authors only rule out bias at first contact. They suggest looking at networks and in-person meetings, which fits Howell & Nanda.

Algorithms widened the pool

Lyonnet & Stern's model picked more women and non-capital-city founders than VCs did. Bartlett et al. found fintech lenders reduced rate gaps.

See Pillars 2 and 3

So the claim to test is narrower: which training data and feedback structure makes AI compound bias versus correct it.

The gap your project fills

The VC papers above are mostly snapshots: one dataset, one decision point. The feedback-loop papers model dynamics, but in policing and credit. In today's search I did not find a study that simulates an AI screener retraining on its own funded cohorts over multiple cycles. That is not proof none exists. Run a proper database search (Google Scholar, SSRN) and ask Dr. Pearl before claiming novelty.

  1. Start from the selective-labels problem: only funded startups get exit labels.
  2. Borrow Ensign et al.'s urn setup and swap in founder groups and exits.
  3. Seed with real gaps (Pillar 1) and a prototype-weighted scorer (Pillar 4).
  4. Run ~5 cycles, then test interventions: numeric traction anchors, exploration quotas, training on unfunded firms too.
  5. Use VC and founder interviews to check which signals screeners actually use.
