Scene 6 · new unit: startups
1of 100

startups a VC firm looks at gets the check.

That's the median, from a survey of about 885 VCs. Most deals arrive through networks, and the team is rated the top reason to invest. With that much filtering, whatever a screener treats as a "good founder" decides almost everything.

Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan & Strebulaev (2020), Journal of Financial Economics
Scene 7 · same pitch, different voice
68vs 32

Out of 100 people choosing between identical pitches, 68 backed the one narrated by a man.

The words were the same. Only the voice changed. Women and men watching made the same choice.

Brooks, Huang, Kearney & Murray (2014), PNAS
Scene 8 · but not everywhere
45vs 41

interested replies per 1,000 cold pitch emails: female names got slightly more.

80,000 fake pitches went to 28,000 investors with randomly assigned founder names. At the inbox, women weren't penalized. The bias in this research shows up later: in meetings, networks and who follows up.

Gornall & Strebulaev (2025), Management Science (4.5% vs 4.1%)
Scene 9 · now add an AI

Train a screener on past winners. Its picks become the next winners.

Funded founders go around the loop and become training data. Rejected founders fall off and never produce an outcome, so the model never learns it was wrong about them. Researchers call this the selective-labels problem.

Lakkaraju et al. (2017); Ensign et al. (2018); Bonelli (2023)
Scene 10 · from your simulator
25vs 48

top-talent women vs. top-talent men funded, per 100, by a prototype-matching AI.

Talent was equal by design. The gap came from a skewed history and a model that funds whoever resembles past winners. It didn't spiral, it locked in.
